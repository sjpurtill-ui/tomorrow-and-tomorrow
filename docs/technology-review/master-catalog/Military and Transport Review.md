# Military research and transport

182 authored drafts: 61 defense and military research, 60 overland transport and 61 navigation and maritime systems. These are proposed discoveries, not implemented units or operating services. The section adds 24 alternative prerequisite groups and keeps one identity when different learning routes reconverge.

The military tree develops several interacting capabilities: equipment, personnel training, command practice, observation, mobility, protection and sustainment. Transport develops several competing networks whose usefulness depends on actual terrain, infrastructure, energy and throughput. Neither branch is a single replacement ladder.

## Military choices that change behavior

| Choice | Proposed distinction | Continuing cost or weakness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sling or javelin screens versus massed archers | Different equipment, training demands, carried ammunition and collective roles | A discovered method does not supply trained people or projectiles; screening and sustained fire are different jobs |
| Foot scouts versus mounted or wheeled scouts | Different travel, observation and return capabilities | Mounts need care and forage; vehicles need fuel, maintainers and usable routes |
| Shielded, mail-equipped or plate-equipped formations | Protection depends on actual coverage, threat and fit | Weight, fatigue, cost and repair differ; no armor gives universal invulnerability |
| Wheeled versus tracked carriers | Different road, ground-contact, support and maintenance envelopes | A reconnaissance vehicle is not automatically a troop carrier; payload and crew spaces must be designed |
| Fighting vehicles versus recovery crews | Recovery can return some disabled equipment to repair instead of adding offensive strength | It needs lifting or towing capacity, trained crews and physical access to the disabled vehicle |
| Crewed versus uncrewed reconnaissance | Different exposure, endurance, sensing and communication arrangements | Platforms remain physical equipment; losses, energy, maintenance and failed links remain consequential |
| Concentrated versus delegated command | Trained subordinates can interpret objectives within authority when contact is delayed | Reports can be stale, intent misunderstood and commands refused; delegation does not create perfect coordination |
| Defensive sensors versus defensive effectors | Observation and identification provide information for actual available units | A radar or counter-drone observer does not automatically intercept an object |

The role targets attached to military records describe intended design families, including support services. They are not additions to the current unit count and are not live archetype identifiers. Several discoveries may support one role, and one discovery may support several roles. Appearance, country labels or small statistical variants do not create additional discovery identities.

## Generals retain execution in every era

The ruler gives objectives, priorities and constraints through conversation. Generals choose routes, deployment, engagement, withdrawal and routine sustainment within the existing command model. Mission-Order Practice improves a specific organizational skill; it is not the discovery that grants generals their authority. Earlier forces already operate through generals.

Learning a doctrine permits teaching and rehearsal. It does not instantly retrain every formation. Readiness must reflect assigned people, available instructors, practical training, issued equipment, delivered ammunition or provisions, maintenance, fatigue and losses. Exercises consume resources; casualties are not research currency.

GovernmentPeopleSystem remains responsible for civic officials and daily civilian labor. Military assignments must transfer actual available people through existing owners. HistoricalFigures continues to represent exceptional contributors and independent field generals. This catalog introduces no duplicate research workforce, direct-cohort command screen or mandatory logistics form.

## Transport branches remain useful together

| Service need | Alternative means | Physical limit that remains |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cross difficult ground | Foot trails, pack animals, sledges, ropeways, selected vehicles | Surface, gradient, weather, load and actual maintenance |
| Cross water | Ferry service, fixed bridge, pontoon crossing or a longer route | Accessible banks, suitable structures or craft, crews, load and queue capacity |
| Move bulk freight | Guided wagonways, powered rail, road vehicles or shipping | Built routes, compatible vehicles, energy, terminals and last-leg delivery |
| Maintain navigation | Local pilotage, dead reckoning, celestial methods, reference time, radar or satellite signals | Actual observations, available references, uncertainty and changing conditions |
| Deliver cold cargo | Qualified road, rail or water carriers | Cooling mechanism, insulation, power, handling and custody all remain necessary |
| Adopt powered traction | Qualified combustion, supplied electric drive or stored electrical energy | The chosen power chain must physically exist; knowledge of a motor alone supplies no energy |

Intermodal Freight Transfer combines existing transport services through real terminals. It does not teleport freight at a network boundary. Pack animals can retain a useful last-leg role beside railways or motor vehicles; old methods are not automatically deleted when another method is discovered.

## Acquiring capabilities from other societies

A missed local opportunity can be recovered by rebuilding the necessary foundations, receiving delivered research, inviting paid specialists, negotiating a partnership, licensing applicable designs or purchasing an actual supplier's service. Each record includes a proposed outside-learning contract, the same destination identity, a local demonstration and retained operating requirements. No route awards instant mastery.

| Route | Required evidence or capacity | Disadvantage |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Follow foreign research | A competent source, a real report or demonstration and a way for it to arrive | Wait for the source's discovery, negotiate access, interpret partial evidence and demonstrate it locally |
| Visiting specialist or scholar | A capable teacher, agreed payment or trade, travel and host capacity | Arrival delay, finite teaching time and continuing dependence until locals can perform the work |
| Partnership | Agreed contributions, actual personnel or facilities and exchanged evidence | Reciprocal costs, coordination delay, shared control and the risk of interrupted access |
| Commissioned research | A capable supplier with funded work capacity and a defined deliverable | May shorten elapsed research by buying capacity, but requires payment, delivery and local validation; success is not guaranteed |
| Licensed equipment or design | Applicable permission, compatible equipment, local workers and required inputs | Fees, training, maintenance and possible dependence on imported parts; design access is not a factory |
| Acquired specimen | An actual device and qualified analysis tools where this is an appropriate route | Acquisition cost, incomplete information, possible specimen damage and substantial local reproduction work |
| Imported service | A supplier with actual staffed transport, training, repair or other operating capacity | Recurring fees, travel or delivery, negotiated access and loss of service if the supplier cannot continue |

A hired transport service consumes its supplier's carrying capacity and actual journey time. Foreign instruction consumes teacher and learner time. A purchased armored vehicle is one delivered piece of equipment; it neither generates a crew nor teaches its entire production chain. Learned knowledge remains after a contract ends, while rights to continued service and access can expire.

Cost coefficients, exact work requirements, eligible specimen mappings and interruption behavior are still implementation work. The proposed route descriptions are not a claim that every recovery path has been balanced or tested.

## Historical grounding

The National Army Museum describes the different work performed by riding, draft and pack animals and a supply chain joining ports, railheads and last-leg transport. That supports differentiated animal roles and mixed transport networks, rather than replacing all animal service when motors appear. [National Army Museum, Horse power in the First World War](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/horse-power-first-world-war).

The National Park Service describes river, canal, rail and road transport existing alongside one another in the Cuyahoga Valley. This informs the coexistence of route types; it does not establish a universal progression for every geography. [National Park Service, Changes in Transportation](https://www.nps.gov/cuva/learn/kidsyouth/changes-in-transportation.htm).

Royal Museums Greenwich describes the longitude problem and marine timekeepers as a distinct navigation development. The catalog separates a maintained time reference from observations, calculation and reliable vessel operation. [Royal Museums Greenwich, Time and Longitude gallery](https://www.rmg.co.uk/royal-observatory/attractions/time-longitude-gallery).

These sources ground selected design distinctions, not every prerequisite or numerical behavior. All historical sequencing, doctrine semantics, capability models and proposed future systems still need broader review. There are no real-world construction specifications or tactical procedures in this catalog.
